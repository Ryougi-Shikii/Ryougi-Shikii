Slide 3 — Problem statement
Why dark images are genuinely hard to fix
2:00 – 4:00

"Here's the fundamental physics. A camera sensor counts photons. In bright light, each pixel accumulates thousands of counts and the signal-to-noise ratio is excellent. In the dark, you're counting dozens of photons — and the random fluctuation in that count is a large fraction of the signal itself. That's shot noise, and it's not a software bug. It's quantum mechanics."

"To compensate, cameras crank up analogue gain — ISO. But gain amplifies noise along with signal. You also get color channel imbalance because sensors aren't equally sensitive across wavelengths at low light. And if the scene has both a lit window and a dark corner, no single global correction fixes both."

"The result is five simultaneous degradations: underexposure, amplified noise, contrast collapse, color distortion, and loss of fine structure. Fix one naively and you often make another worse. Brighten everything — noise explodes. Suppress noise — edges smear. That's the fundamental tension this paper is about."

Think of it like trying to fix a blurry, underexposed photo by guessing what the original scene looked like — except you have to do it differently for the bright spots than the dark spots, all at the same time.
Slide 4 — Related work part 1
The classical toolkit — fast but brittle
4:00 – 5:30

"The field spent decades on signal-processing approaches. Histogram Equalization — HE — asks: what if we redistributed the brightness values so every level appears equally often? Fast. Simple. Runs in under a millisecond. But it treats the whole image as one flat pool. A face in a dark room and a streetlight behind it get the same treatment, and the result is washed-out, over-enhanced garbage."

"CLAHE — which Pizer introduced in 1987 — fixes this by doing HE locally in tiles. Better. Still gets tile-boundary artefacts. Still can't recover detail that the sensor literally didn't capture."

"Gamma correction applies a power-law curve to intensities. One-line implementation. Works surprisingly well on uniformly underexposed images. Fails the moment you have mixed lighting — which is every real scene."

"And then there's Retinex — Land's 1977 theory of human colour perception. The key insight: your eye separates how bright something is from what color it actually is. Mathematically, the image I equals reflectance R times illumination L, element-wise. If you can estimate L, you can divide it out and get clean R. Elegant theory. The problem is estimating L. Classical solvers use spatial smoothness priors — they assume illumination changes slowly. It doesn't. A lamp casts a hard shadow. And the residual noise ends up amplified in R."

Key table: CLAHE gets 17.10 PSNR, classical Retinex gets 16.63. Both under 17.2 dB. We need to do much better.
Slide 5 — Related work part 2
The deep learning era — and where Transformers enter
5:30 – 7:00

"The first deep learning move was LLNet in 2017 — a stacked sparse autoencoder. It proved you could learn enhancement from data. 17.96 PSNR. Better than classical, but not dramatically."

"RetinexNet — Wei et al., 2018 — did something important: it embedded the Retinex decomposition inside a CNN and released the LOL benchmark dataset that the whole field now trains on. But the CNN still had trouble with globally varying illumination because CNNs have finite receptive fields. A 3×3 convolutional kernel can only see a tiny patch of the image at once."

"EnlightenGAN, Zero-DCE — clever training strategies, weaker metrics. URetinexNet and SNR-Aware pushed to 21 dB. Then Transformers arrived. Restormer hit 22.43. Retiformer — the closest prior work to ours — fused Retinex with Transformer window-attention and hit 22.80."

"So the trend is clear. Every time someone adds global attention to Retinex-based decomposition, numbers go up. Our question was: is there something more principled we can do with that attention signal? And the answer is yes — you can condition the attention on how dark each pixel actually is."

Slide 6 — Architecture overview
RITformer — the big picture in 30 seconds
7:00 – 8:00

"Here's the headline before I go into the details. RITformer is a four-step pipeline. Step one: split the dark image into two layers — the illumination map and the reflectance map. Step two: estimate, for every pixel, how aggressively we need to brighten it. That estimate is a number we call α — the illumination factor. Step three: feed the illumination map into a Transformer that is literally modulated by α at every attention step. Step four: clean the reflectance, then multiply it back with the enhanced illumination."

"The whole model is a U-Net shaped encoder-decoder with Swin Transformer blocks, about 18.4 million parameters — smaller than most comparable restorers."

If you explain nothing else, explain this: α is a map that tells each pixel "how much do you need to be fixed?" Dark pixels get high α. Already-bright pixels get α near zero. The Transformer then pays more attention in dark regions and almost ignores bright ones. That's the whole idea.
Slide 7 — RDN deep-dive
Step 1: Retinex decomposition network
8:00 – 9:30

"The Retinex Decomposition Network — RDN — takes the low-light image and produces two outputs: R-hat, the reflectance, and L-hat, the illumination map."

"It's a lightweight encoder: depthwise separable convolutions, instance normalization. Fast and parameter-efficient. The loss it minimizes has two terms. First, the reconstruction loss — R-hat times L-hat should reproduce the input image. Second, a spatial gradient smoothness penalty on L-hat — we want the illumination to vary slowly and smoothly, not jump around pixel by pixel."

"In plain terms: RDN is answering the question 'which parts of this image are dark because the scene is dim, and which parts are dark because the object itself is dark?' A black cat in a bright room should have a bright illumination map and a dark reflectance. The same black cat in a dim room should have a dim illumination map and still a dark reflectance. Getting this decomposition right is foundational — every downstream step depends on it."

Slide 8 — IFE deep-dive
Step 2: the illumination factor estimator — the core innovation
9:30 – 11:30

"This is the part I want you to really lock onto. Most enhancement models treat illumination correction as a uniform operation — brighten everything according to some learned rule. The problem is that a scene almost never has uniform darkness. You have a face in a shadow, a window in the background, a lamp in the corner."

"IFE takes L-hat and produces α — a spatial field, same height and width as the image, where every pixel has a value between 0 and 1. Low α: 'this pixel is already bright enough, don't do much.' High α: 'this pixel is in the dark, enhance aggressively.'"

"It's a three-layer depthwise separable convolutional network with a sigmoid output. Tiny. Fast. But it produces the control signal that drives everything downstream."

"Think of it like a dimmer switch for every single pixel independently. Your bedroom has a dimmer — you can make the room uniformly brighter. IFE gives every pixel its own dimmer, set exactly as bright as it needs to be and no brighter. That's how we prevent over-brightening highlights while recovering severely dark areas."

Ablation result: removing α drops PSNR by 2.3 dB — the single largest contribution of any component.
Slide 9 — IGTE deep-dive
Step 3: illumination-guided Transformer enhancer
11:30 – 13:30

"Now we get to the Transformer. The IGTE tokenizes L-hat into 8×8 patches, projects them to 256 dimensions, and passes them through 6 Illumination-Guided Self-Attention blocks — IGSA."

"The key modification to standard self-attention is this. In a normal Transformer, the query vector Q is compared to every key K to produce attention weights. In IGSA, we scale each query by its corresponding α value before that comparison. The equation is: IGSA equals softmax of α times Q times K-transpose over root-dk, times V."

"What does that do? In dark regions, α is large. Scaling Q up makes the dot product with K larger, which means after softmax the attention weights spread more broadly across the image. The model is looking far and wide for context in dark areas — trying to understand the global illumination structure. In bright areas, α is small, the attention stays local and focused. The model is saying 'I know what's happening here, I don't need to look far.'"

"After the Transformer, the enhanced illumination L-hat-star is the original plus the Transformer correction. Clean, residual formulation — if the Transformer learned nothing, the output is still the original illumination map. The network can only add, not corrupt."

Imagine a security guard watching a camera feed. In well-lit areas they glance around calmly. In dark areas they lean forward and scan the whole frame intensely. α is the guard's alertness dial. That's IGSA.
Slide 10 — Reflectance refiner
Step 4: cleaning the reflectance and rebuilding the image
13:30 – 14:30

"Remember that decomposition amplifies noise in the reflectance layer. The Reflectance Refiner is a three-level U-Net with residual connections that denoises R-hat — suppressing sensor noise without blurring texture."

"Final reconstruction is simply R-hat-star element-wise multiplied by L-hat-star. We also have a global residual from input to output to ease gradient flow during training."

"The composite training loss has four terms: Charbonnier pixel loss for reconstruction fidelity, VGG-19 perceptual loss for natural appearance, multi-scale SSIM for structural preservation, and an illumination consistency loss that penalizes the estimated illumination map for being physically implausible. Weights are 1.0, 0.1, 0.2, and 0.15 respectively — empirically tuned."

Slide 12 — Ablation study
What's actually doing the work?
16:30 – 17:30

"We ran an ablation to isolate each component. Remove α — revert to unguided Transformer attention — and PSNR drops 2.3 dB. That is the largest single contribution of any component in the model. The illumination factor is not a minor tweak; it is the central mechanism."

"Remove the Transformer entirely and replace with a CNN illumination enhancer: minus 1.8 dB. This confirms that global attention for illumination estimation is genuinely valuable — a CNN with its limited receptive field can't capture the relationship between a dark foreground and a bright background window across the whole image."

"Remove the reflectance refinement U-Net: minus 1.5 dB. Remove the illumination consistency loss: minus 0.5 dB. The full model requires all four components — but α is where you should focus if you're thinking about follow-up work."

Slide 13 — Qualitative & generalisation
What does it actually look like? And does it transfer?
17:30 – 18:30

"CLAHE's failure mode is tile-boundary blocking — you can see the grid if you look. Classical Retinex produces halo artefacts at bright edges because its illumination estimate doesn't perfectly track scene boundaries. Gamma correction desaturates colors on mixed-illumination scenes."

"RITformer eliminates all three. No halos — because the smoothness prior on the illumination map is learned, not fixed. No tile artefacts — there are no tiles. No desaturation — the reflectance pathway preserves colour while the Transformer handles brightness."

"On ExDark generalisation — 100 images across 12 object categories, no paired reference, evaluated by NIQE — RITformer achieves the lowest score in 10 of 12 categories. The two weak spots are candlelight scenes. Makes sense: LOL doesn't have much candlelight training data, and extreme spectral imbalance from a single compact warm source is genuinely out-of-distribution. We have preliminary results showing domain-adaptive fine-tuning on a small candlelight subset closes most of that gap."

Slide 14 — Future work & close
Where RITformer goes next
18:30 – 20:00

"Four directions we're most excited about. First — richer conditioning. Right now α is a scalar field. What if we conditioned it on scene semantics? A face needs different enhancement treatment than a road sign. An illumination embedding that knows what's in the scene could unlock another level of adaptivity."

"Second — diffusion sampling for perceptual diversity. PSNR-optimal outputs converge toward pixel-level averages. Diffusion priors could let us sample plausible high-frequency texture instead. This is particularly relevant for surveillance applications where you want the sharpest plausible reconstruction."

"Third — mobile deployment. 18.4 million parameters is already lean. A knowledge-distilled variant targeting smartphone NPUs is straightforward and immediately commercially relevant."

"Fourth — training data. The LOL v1 benchmark has 485 training pairs. That's tiny. Building or licensing paired training sets that cover autonomous driving night scenarios and clinical low-dose imaging would dramatically broaden deployment."

"To close: the core contribution here is simple. We asked 'can we teach a Transformer to know where it needs to work hardest and scale its attention accordingly?' The answer is yes, and α is the mechanism. 23.6 dB, 18.4 million parameters, 48 milliseconds. Thank you."

End on the one-sentence summary: "We gave every pixel its own brightness dial, and taught a Transformer to read it." That's RITformer.
